Distributed Denial of Service (DDoS) attack

DDos is a malicious cyberattack that aims to make an online service or website unavailable by overwhelming it with a flood of illegitimate traffic originating from multiple compromised computer systems.

🆚 DoS vs. DDoS Attack

The key difference between a Denial-of-Service (DoS) and a Distributed Denial-of-Service (DDoS) attack lies in the number of attack sources.

Feature Denial-of-Service (DoS) Attack Distributed Denial-of-Service (DDoS) Attack
Attack Source Single computer system. Multiple compromised computer systems (a botnet).
Traffic Volume Generally lower, limited by the capacity of a single machine. Significantly higher, capable of overwhelming large networks.
Detection Easier to detect and block, as the source IP address is singular. Much harder to detect and mitigate due to the traffic coming from many distributed, seemingly legitimate sources.
Speed Slower to deploy and less intense. Can be deployed rapidly, increasing its damage potential.

In short: A DDoS attack is a more powerful and harder-to-defend version of a DoS attack.

🎯 Purpose of a DDoS Attack

The primary purpose of a DDoS attack is to cause a denial of service—to make a website, network, or online service unusable for its legitimate users.

Common motivations include:

  • Extortion: Demanding a ransom to stop the attack.
  • Hacktivism: Protesting or making a political/ideological statement against an organization.
  • Vandalism/Disruption: Simply causing chaos, disruption, or revenge against a company or entity.
  • Competitive Advantage: Sabotaging a competitor's services, such as during a critical sales event.
  • Distraction: Using the DDoS attack as a cover to launch a secondary, more sinister cybercrime (like data exfiltration or malware deployment) while the IT team is focused on mitigating the service denial.

⚙️ How a DDoS Attack Works

A DDoS attack typically involves two main stages:

1. Creating the Botnet

The attacker creates a botnet, which is a network of internet-connected devices (computers, servers, IoT devices, etc.) that have been infected with malware called a bot (short for robot). The owners of these devices are usually unaware that their systems have been compromised. The attacker controls this entire network of "zombie" devices remotely via a Command and Control (C2) server.

2. Launching the Attack

The attacker sends a command to the botnet. All infected devices simultaneously begin flooding the target's IP address (server or network) with an overwhelming volume of requests or malicious data packets.

Since the target server can only handle a finite number of requests and maintain a limited number of connections, the massive surge of junk traffic consumes all its available bandwidth, processing power, and memory. This resource exhaustion prevents the server from responding to legitimate user requests, resulting in a system slowdown or complete crash—a denial of service.

🚨 Common Types of DDoS Attacks

DDoS attacks are categorized based on which layer of the network connection (OSI model) they target:

1. Volumetric Attacks (Layer 3 & 4)

These attacks aim to consume all available bandwidth between the target and the rest of the internet by flooding the network with massive amounts of traffic.

  • Example: UDP Flood or ICMP Flood (sending a high volume of ping requests).

2. Protocol Attacks (Layer 3 & 4)

These attacks exploit weaknesses in network communication protocols, aiming to exhaust the resources of network infrastructure components like firewalls, load balancers, and servers.

  • Example: SYN Flood—The attacker initiates a massive number of incomplete TCP connection requests (the "three-way handshake" is never completed), filling up the server's connection table and preventing legitimate new connections.

3. Application Layer Attacks (Layer 7)

These are the most sophisticated attacks, targeting the specific application layer where the user interacts with the website (e.g., HTTP requests). They are often low-volume but focus on consuming server resources by requesting pages that require heavy computational effort to process.

  • Example: HTTP Flood—The attacker sends numerous seemingly legitimate HTTP GET or POST requests to the web server, overwhelming its resources.

🏢 Real-Life Analogy

Imagine a popular retail store on a Saturday morning with a limited number of service counter staff.

  • Legitimate Customers: People who want to enter the store, browse, and make a purchase.
  • The Store's Staff/Resources: The server's capacity to handle requests.

DoS Analogy

One aggressive person stands at the store entrance, repeatedly running back and forth or blocking the door. This single person makes it difficult or impossible for legitimate customers to enter, but security can easily isolate and remove this single source of disruption.

DDoS Analogy

Suddenly, thousands of people (the botnet) flood the store. They don't buy anything; they just walk in, ask the staff complex, meaningless questions, stand shoulder-to-shoulder, and generally clog all aisles and the counter. Security cannot simply remove one person; the sheer distributed volume of the crowd overwhelms the staff and resources, making it impossible for real customers to get service.

🛡️ Defense Against DDoS

Defense against DDoS attacks typically requires a multi-layered strategy focused on detection, diversion, and mitigation.

  • DDoS Protection Services: Utilizing specialized cloud-based DDoS mitigation providers (like Cloudflare, Akamai, AWS Shield). These services have massive bandwidth and sophisticated tools to absorb and filter malicious traffic before it reaches the target network.
  • Rate Limiting: Implementing controls to restrict the number of requests a server will accept from any specific IP address within a short timeframe.
  • Blackholing/Sinkholing: Routing all traffic to the targeted IP address into a "black hole," where it is dropped. This stops the attack but also denies service to legitimate users, making it a last-resort option.
  • Content Delivery Networks (CDNs): Using a CDN distributes content across multiple servers worldwide. This diffuses the attack surface, making it harder to overwhelm a single point, and often caches content so origin servers don't have to process every request.
  • Upstream Filtering and Scrubbing Centers: ISPs and specialized vendors can analyze incoming traffic and "scrub" (filter) out malicious packets based on known attack signatures, passing only clean traffic through to the destination.
